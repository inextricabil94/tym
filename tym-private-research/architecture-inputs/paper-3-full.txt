Abstract —This  report  presents  the  practical  results  obtained  in  the  development  of  a  technology  that  supports  the  Time  Yards  Model.  Time  segmentation  is  realised  using   CNNs.  Different  techniques  and  results  are  presented  in  comparison,  underlining  their  main  strengths  and  weaknesses.  
Keywords—formalism,  time,  temporality,  named  entity  recognition,  geoparsing,  recurrent  neural  network,  natural  text  processing   
I.  M
OTIVATION
 The  main  focus  of  the  research  described  in  this  paper  is  to  offer  a  technical  solution  that  implements  the  Time  Yards  Model  (TYM),  which  means  to  put  in  evidence  time  tracks  in  a  book  text.  This  paper  has  as  a  ground  foundation  an  already  manually  annotated  novel  that  is  used  to  train  a  Convolutional  Neural  Network  (CNN)  in  order  to  identify  time  tracks.  Several  text  processing  techniques  are  described  and  a  comparison  between  different  text  processing  frameworks  is  made.  A  few  approaches  regarding  time  segmentations  are  proposed  and  evaluated.   
II.  D
EFINITIONS
  The  following  definitions  clarify  terms  frequently  used  in  TYM  and  which  will  be  referred  to  repeatedly  in  this  report.  A.  Events   The  notion  of  event  is  close  to  that  described  in  TimeML  (Pustejovsky,  2005),  being  mainly  attributed  to  verbs  and  deverbatives.  Syntactically,  an  event  is  realised  at  the  level  of  a  clause  of  a  sentence,  i.e.  a  word  sequence  around  a  main  active  verb  or  a  verb  compound.  Semantically,  an  event  mainly  exposes  an  action  in  which  a  verb  is  surrounded  by  roles,  such  as  agent,  recipient,  etc.,  whose  syntactic  realisations  in  the  clause  are:  subject,  direct  object,  obliques,  etc.   Although  not  always  explicitly  expressed,  events  are  positioned  on  the  story  time  axis,  either  reported  to  time  moments  or  intervals  (year,  month,  week,  day,  hour,  Christmas,  Thanksgiving,  etc.),  or  to  other  events,  and  then,  they  can  be  simultaneous,  partially  intersecting,  placed  before  or  after  -  see  (Allen,  1994).  However,  in  many  cases  there  is  no  concrete  positioning  of  an  event  with  respect  to  a  temporal  expression  denoting  a  date  or  a  moment  or  to  another  event.  As  such,  events  that  make  up  the  semantic  content  of  a  

===PAGE 2===
book  can  be  considered  as  being  partially  ordered  on  the  story  time  axis.    Further,  usually,  in  events  entities  are  involved.  These  can  be  book  characters,  objects,  abstract  ideas,  etc.  In  TYM,  persons  (characters  of  a  book)  are  of  particular  interest.  They  fulfil  different  verb  roles,  some  being  more  important  than  others.  In  our  analysis  we  are  concerned  mainly  with  the  semantic  roles  of  agents  and  recipients  (i.e.  fulfilling  syntactic  positions  of  subjects,  direct  object  and  indirect  object  with  respect  to  a  main  verb.   B.  Time  Segments  (TSs)   At  the  basis  of  our  model  conceptualising  a  practical  approach  towards  for  TYM  and  of  central  focus  in  this  paper  is  the  notion  of  Time  Segment  (TS),  which,  conforming  to  (Cristea  &  Macovei,  2017;  Macovei,  2021),  also  detailed  in  (Boghiu,  2020),  represents  a  continuous  span  of  the  book  text  or  a  sequence  of  clauses  observing  three  constraints:  the  unity  of  time,  of  characters  and  of  perspective.    The  unity  of  time  refers  to  the  fact  that  the  time  span  on  the  story  time  axis  of  the  events  that  make  up  the  TS  are  either  synchronous  or  are  contiguous  in  their  time  sequentiality.    The  unity  of  characters  refers  to  the  fact  that  the  characters  fulfilling  the  main  roles  (agent,  recipient)  are  the  constant  all  along  the  TS.   The  unity  of  perspective  refers  to  the  fact  that  the  events  constituting  the  TS  are  told  by  the  same  narrator  (the  author  or  one  of  the  book  characters).  C.  Time  Tracks  (TTs)  By  slightly  reinterpreting  the  original  definitions  (Cristea  &  Macovei,  2017;  Macovei,  2021),  a  TT  can  be  described  as  a  maximal,  sequence  of  time  segments  partially  ordered  with  respect  to  the  story  time  axis,  in  which  a  stable  set  of  characters  is  involved  and  whose  stories  are  told  from  a  unique  
perspective.  The  restriction  of  being  maximal  refers  to  the  story  time  axis:  a  time  track  should  include  the  “destiny”  of  a  set  of  characters  extended  to  the  whole  span  of  the  book  text.  The  restriction  referring  to  a  stable  set  of  characters  is  a  selectional  restriction  and  not  one  that  touches  the  semantic  content  of  the  text,  because  it  is  known  that,  in  a  text,  characters  meet  and  separate,  interfere  and  split  continuously  during  the  development  of  the  story.  As  such,  a  multitude  of  time  tracks  could  be  interrogated  about  a  text,  with  respect  to  different  sets  of  characters  there  involved  and,  in  particular,  a  time  track  can  be  searched  for  only  one  book  hero.  The  notion  of  time  track  has  a  lot  in  common  with  that  of  narrative  thread  and,  by  following  just  one  character,  one  can  perceive  this  hero’s  involvement  in  other  characters’  destinies,  as  depicted  by  the  author.  Let’s  notice  also  that  the  sequence  of  TSs  constituting  a  TT  may  leave  uncovered  stretches  of  time  on  the  story  time  axis,  therefore  unknown  developments  from  the  destinies  of  the  corresponding  characters,  segments  of  time  in  their  told  lives  about  which  the  narrator  does  not  say  anything.   As  said,  each  TT  is  attached  to  a  specific  set  of  characters  and  a  certain  narrator.  Along  one  TT  attached  to  a  declared  set  of  characters ,  specific  points  can  be  put  in  evidence:   -  the  start  point :  its  leftmost  point  on  the  story  time  axis.  This  is  where  the  characters  in  the  declared  set  are  mentioned  together  for  the  first  time  in  the  novel,   -  the  end  point :  its  rightmost  point  on  the  story  time  axis.  This  is  where  the  characters  in  the  declared  set  are  mentioned  together  for  the  last  time  in  the  novel:  -  a  split  point  of  a  TT:  an  interior  point  within  the  time  span  of  the  novel  story  in  which  the  current  TT  is  interrupted  for  the  reason  that  its  specific  set  of  characters  split,  at  least  one  

===PAGE 3===
character  from  the  set  exiting  the  set.  A  specific  type  of  split  point,  which  we  will  call  an  interruption  point ,  refers  to  situations  where  the  author  temporally  interrupts  the  narration  on  the  specific  set  of  characters.  As  such,  a  split  point  of  a  TT  attached  to  one  character  only  is  always  an  interruption  point,  because  it  represents  a  point  on  the  story  time  axis  where  the  author  interrupts  the  narration  about  that  particular  character;   -  a  merge  point :  an  interior  point  within  the  time  span  of  the  novel  story  in  which  the  current  TT  meets  another  TT  specific  to  another  set  of  characters.  This  represents  a  point  in  the  story  where  the  set  of  characters  specific  to  the  current  TT  meet  with  at  least  another  character.  A  specific  type  of  merge  point,  which  we  will  call  a  restore  point ,  refers  to  situations  where  an  interrupted  narration  on  the  specific  set  of  characters  is  restored. URSUIT
  The  logical  pursuit  in  implementing  this  model  is  as  follows:   1).  identify  events,  which  is  to  find  clause  boundaries;  2).  put  in  evidence  temporal  relations,  either  of  an  event  with  respect  to  a  fixed  temporal  borne  or  between  events;   3).  put  in  evidence  the  set  of  characters  (person  type  entities)  participating  in  an  event;  4).  put  in  evidence  the  perspective  from  which  a  certain  event  is  told;  5).  identify  time  segment  boundaries,  as  a  continuous  sequence  (in  the  length  of  the  text)  of  events  in  which  the  time  flows  sequentially  and/or  is  stationary;  6).  detect  the  TT  of  a  given  set  of  characters  (as  dictated  by  the  user),  in  two  steps:  make  a  selection  among  the  detected  set  of  TSs  for  those  which  include  the  given  set  and  reorder  
this  selected  set  of  TSs  with  respect  to  their  time  spans.    In  the  following,  we  will  briefly  describe  each  of  these  steps  from  an  implementation  point  of  view.   A.  Event  Segmentation    Events  are  the  main  brick  in  composing  time  segments.  At  the  level  of  the  text,  an  event  is  offered  by  a  syntactic  construct  built  around  a  verb,  in  which  proper  nouns,  personal  pronouns,  etc.  participate  in  the  position  of  subject,  direct  objects  and  other  syntactic  roles.  In  order  to  detect  basic  structures  such  as  events,  the  processes  of  tokenization  and  POS-tagging  are  realized  for  each  sentence  using  the  NLTK  framework
 1
,  being  immediately  followed  by  chunking.  The  tokens  are  characterised  by  their  part  of  speeches,  while  chunking  offers  the  possibility  to  define  patterns  that  make  up  constituents  of  the  structure  of  the  event.  Such  patterns  are  described  in  more  detail  in  chapter  IV.    Event  detection  comes  after  performing  the  preprocessing  phase,  where  we  extract  all  the  needed  information  in  order  to  build  a  basic  event  (character,  temporal  expression,  location,  action).  All  events  must  have  a  set  of  characters  assigned,  which  can  be  specifically  mentioned  in  the  sentence,  deduced  by  a  coreference  resolution  module,  or  inferred  based  on  heuristics.  When  no  character  of  type  person  is  mentioned  in  an  event,  considering  the  same  set  of  characters  as  of  the  previous  event  in  sequence,  can  be  a  successful  heuristics,  which  we  have  adopted.  Same  logic  is  applied  for  temporal  expressions.  They  must  be  fully  anchored  (by  specific  temporal  expressions  of  type  date,  moment,  etc.)  or  they

take  over  the  last  identified  temporal  anchor  from  previous  events  (the  current  event  will  be  immediately  after  the  previous  one  on  a  time  axis).    Events  are  detected  in  an  initial  phase  using  patterns  for  chunking,  but  they  go  through  a  second  test  where  key  words  from  the  specific  list  of  conjunctions  are  identified  in  the  sentence.  A  third  test  is  made  when  no  conjunction  is  found,  but  many  verbs  were  present  in  the  sentence.  In  this  case  delimiter  punctuation  signs  are  identified,  such  as:  , ,  . ,  - ,  … ,  ; .  If  no  such  sign  is  identified,  the  events  are  splitted  in  the  close  proximity  of  the  second  verb.  The  described  steps  only  apply  for  sentences  with  multiple  verbs.  In  cases  where  a  sentence  contains  only  one  verb  then  a  single  event  equal  to  the  whole  sentence  is  extracted.    This  algorithm  for  detecting  event  in  multiple  verb  sentences  can  be  described  by  the  following  pseudocode:   1:  chunks  =  GetChunks(sentence)  2:  if(chunks.length  >  0)  3:  SplitEventsByConjInChunks(chunks);  4:  else   if  (ConjFromListFound(sentence))  5:  SplitEventsByConjInList(sentence);  6:  else  if  (PunctSignFound(sentence))  7:  SplitEventsByPunctSign(sentence);  8:  else  SplitEventsByVerbs(sentence);   
B.  Identifying  temporal  relations   Once  the  events  are  individualised,  they  should  be  placed  in  temporal  order.  Important  clues  in  establishing  relations  of  time  precedence  or  succession  between  events  are  given  by  the  subordinating  conjunctions.  They  provide  a  transition  between  two  ideas  in  the  same  sentence  that  always  indicates  a  temporal  relationship,  a  spatial  relationship  or  a  cause  to  effect  relationship.  
 Temporal  expressions  associated  prove  to  be  very  helpful  in  identifying  temporal  anchoring  of  events.  There  are  more  cases  to  consider  in  time-anchoring  the  current  event
 2
:  -  If  the  current  event  contains  a  temporal  expression  of  type  interval,  then  this  event  should  be  attributed  inside  this  temporal  interval,  as  in  the  following  example:  1.  Last  week  John  played  tennis  with  Bill.  Unit  1  describes  an  event  which  is  temporally  anchored  by  the  interval-type  temporal  expression  last  week .   -  If  no  temporal  expression  is  contained  in  the  current  event,  the  time  of  the  event  is  considered  to  be  contingent  or  immediately  following  the  one  of  the  previous  event,  as  in  the  following  example:  1.  Last  week  John  played  tennis  with  Bill.  2.  He  won  by  3  to  2 .  Here  the  event  is  unit  2  follows  the  one  in  unit  1.   On  the  other  hand,  coordinating  and  correlative  conjunctions  are  used  to  connect  events  of  the  same  importance,  which  are  usually  simultaneous.   Conjunctive  adverbs  may  not  necessarily  express  space  or  time  relationships  between  events,  but  are  clues  indicating  their  belonging  to  the  same  TS.    The  considered  levels  of  annotation  are  referring  to:   -  phrase  annotation  (in  an  incipient  phase);  -  lexical  and  morphological  annotation  (as  revealed  by  a  tokenizer,  a  lemmatizer  and  a  POS-tagger);  -  time  annotation  as  revealed  by  packages  such  as  Duckling,  SUTime  and  others.  The  mentioned  conjunctions  are  used  to  connect  temporal  events  and  to  offer  more  significance  to  isolated  temporal  expressions  found  by  libraries.

EXTRACTION
   In  order  to  automatically  detect  time  segments  and  build  time  tracks  we  have  used  a  corpus  already  annotated  to  TYM  (Macovei,  2021).  Apart  from  this,  a  preprocessing  phase  is  necessary  to  provide  the  elementary  features  needed  to  the  learning  phase  of  a  neural  network  approach.  Not  only  a  few  libraries  are  used,  but  many,  and  the  results  are  merged  together  for  two  reasons:  in  many  cases  some  libraries  may  have  better  results  than  others  and  it  is  often  the  case  that  features  must  be  combined  in  order  to  provide  more  details.  The  preprocessing  phase  extracts  from  the  raw  text  the  details  of  information  necessary  to  perform  event  detection  and  segmentation  into  TSs.
 
A.  The  preprocessing  phase   A  first  step  in  data  preprocessing  is  to  split  the  text  into  tokens,  sentences,  to  notate  parts  of  speeches  and  to  identify  chunks,  tasks  realised  (for  English)  with  help  from  the  NLTK  framework.  This  is  very  helpful  in  developing  the  rule-based  and  dictionary-based  modules  for  extracting  temporal  expressions  and  events  and  building  the  corresponding  time  tracks.  We  have  chosen  NLTK  because  it  identifies  not  only  the  part  of  speeches  of  tokens  but  also  their  relevant  morpho-syntactic  information.  For  example,  for  verbs  it  identifies  the  person  and  the  tense,  while  for  conjunctions  it  identifies  if  it’s  a  coordinating  or  a  subordinating  one.  This  framework  comes  with  the  possibility  of  creating  chunking  rules  that  prove  very  helpful  in  detecting  and  linking  events.    For  example,  a  sentence  may  contain  several  clauses  linked  or  not  by  conjunctions,

with  the  same  tenses  or  not.  A  first  attempt  in  transforming  text  in  numerical  representations  was  made  using  the  frequency  vectorizer.  The  main  idea  is  to  create  a  dictionary  with  all  the  distinct  words  found  in  text  and  compute  for  each  one  the  number  of  occurrences.  This  can  be  done  at  the  whole  text  level  or  just  at  the  sentence  level.  The  problem  with  this  implementation  is  the  size  of  the  dictionary  when  it  comes  to  processing  large  texts  as  well  as  the  useless  number  of  zeros  stored  for  words  that  may  appear  very  rarely  with  a  very  negative  impact  on  performance.  A  solution  to  this  problem  was  to  include  only  relevant  words,  such  as  nouns,  verbs  and  conjunctions  that  provide  valuable  information.  Although  the  computational  time  and  dictionary  size  decreased  significantly,  the  model  did  not  represent  the  data  in  a  manner  that  can  be  reliable  for  future  testing.    The  solution  to  this  problem  came  when  using  TF-IDF  (Mingyong   &  Jiangang,  2012).  The  main  idea  is  that  some  words  may  be  found  very  often  but  do  not  necessarily  offer  significant  information,  so  the  problem  is  solved  by  normalizing  the  term  frequency  taking  into  account  the  whole  text.  With  other  words,  if  a  word  is  found  very  often  in  a  sentence  (or  time  segment)  but  is  found  rarely  in  others,  then  the  term  is  much  more  significant.  For  example,  a  character  may  be  found  mentioned  multiple  times  in  a  sentence  (explicitly  or  identified  by  coreference  resolution),  but  more  rarely  in  others,  then  it  gains  more  importance.   After  computing  the  corresponding  table  for  all  significant  terms  (nouns,  verbs,  conjunctions)  only  the  most  significant  ones  have  been  kept  (the  chosen  TF-IDF  value  had  to  be  >  0.7).   
 The  TF-IDF  method  was  used  to  find  the  most  significant  terms  that  must  be  embedded  later  in  the  word2vec  format  (Ying  Xie  et.  al,  2018).  This  format  is  widely  used  as  a  model  for  neural  networks  and  the  focus  of  this  article  will  be  on  the  classification  task  using  a  Convolutional  Neural  Network  (CNN)  (Amit  Kumar,  2020).   Other  features  have  been  added  as  well,  being  mostly  composed  of  counts:  ●  Tense  of  verbs  in  a  time  segment  -  quantified  as  1  =  Past,  2  =  Present,  3  =  Future;  ●  Number  of  verbs;  ●  Number  of  conjunctions  for  each  type  -  quantified  as  1  =  coordinating  conjunction,  2  =  subordinating  conjunction;  ●  Number  of  characters;  ●  Counts  of  associated  temporal  expressions  -  quantified  as  numbers  of:  1  =  precedence,  2  =  simultaneity,  3  =  succession;  ●  Difference  between  associated  temporal  expressions  from  different  TS  where  the  relation  from  TS1  and  TS2  equals  to  BEFORE  or  IMMEDIATELY_BEFORE  quantified  as:  1  =  precedence,  2  =  simultaneity,  3  =  succession  -  the  expected  result  should  be  1  -  same  logic  is  applied  for  other  relation  types.    The  training  set  consisted  of  376  time  segments  and  382  time  segment  relations  for  the  English  language.  Because  the  size  of  the  annotated  TSs  in  the  corpus  is  clearly  insufficient  for  training  a  neural  network  that  requires  at  least  a  few  thousands  data  points,  we  have  considered  the  extracted  events  from  each  TS  as  training  data  points  since  the  text  from  each  segment  was  of  a  decent  length.  As  a  result,  we  have  extracted  over  10,000  events.

VI.  R
ESULTS
  Identifying  time  segments  was  realized  using  the  classifications  retrieved  from  the  CNN   for  every  event.  Every  extracted  event  from  the  testing  set  has  the  same  features  computed  as  the  events  in  the  training  data  set.  The  goal  is  to  classify  each  one  using  one  of  the  categories:  Past,  Present,  Future.    Time  segments  are  then  obtained  by  concatenating  events  of  the  same  category  and  in  ascending  order  of  their  starting  and  ending  position  (offset)  in  text  until  the  category  changes.  This  is  the  point  that  indicates  that  another  time  axis  starts,  so  another  time  segment  should  be  delimited.    Time  tracks  can  easily  be  obtained  further  by  comparing  the  temporal  expressions  associated  with  the  time  segments:  compare  the  first  identified  temporal  expression  from  TS1  with  the  last  identified  temporal  expressions  from  TS2  in  order  to  establish  one  of  the  relations:  Before,  Simultaneous,  After. VII.  C
ONCLUSIONS
  Most  NLP  frameworks  performed  well,  identifying  almost  the  same  number  of  temporal  expressions,  named  entities  and  organizations.  Although  ANNIE  GATE  offers  great  support  in  english  and  their  data  structures  are  easier  to  use,  we  recommend  SpaCy  for  extending  the  language  portfolio.   Although  the  training  set  was  very  thin,  we  managed  to  overcome  this  obstacle  by  using  events  for  classification  instead  of  time  segments.  For  better  precision  in  future  studies,  a  training  set  consisting  of  a  few  thousands  time  segments  is  required.  The  classification  method  is  also  highly  influenced  by  the  selected  features,  so  we  recommend  a  feature  selection  step  to  identify  which  ones  are  relevant  or  not.
