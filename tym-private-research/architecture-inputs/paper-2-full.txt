Abstract —This  document  identifies  state  of  the  art  techniques  that  can  be  used  in  order  to  formalise  a  general  theory  of  action  and  time.  The  main  goal  of  this  paper  is  to  analyze  existing  formalisms  that  describe  actions,  events  and  meanings  found  in  texts.  In  this  context,  developing  a  formalism  that  can  process  the  text  not  only  syntactically,  but  also  from  a  temporal  and  spatial  point  of  view  is  required.  
This  report  makes  a  review  of  prominent  models  and  techniques  used  in  Natural  Language  Processing  applications  that  deal  with  time  and  space.  Our  investigation  is  intended  to  draw  the  basic  knowledge  level  over  which  a  research  intended  to  decipher  in  texts  the  occurrence  of  events,  together  with  their  involved  characters,  and  their  relation  with  time  and  space.  
Keywords—formalism,  action,  time,  temporality,  named  entity  recognition,  geoparsing,  natural  text  processing    
I.  I
NTRODUCTION
  Most  Natural  Language  Processing  formalisms  used  today  focus  on  how  time  and  space  are  expressed  in  text  do  make  use  of  some  common  features,  such  as  time  and  space  measures  in  different  units  or  directly  expressed  temporal  and  spatial  correlations,  out  of  which  partial  ordering  of  events  in  time  or  spatial  arrangements  of  objects  can  be  inferred  or  predicted.  Other  very  recent  techniques  get  rid  of  specific  features  at  all  and,  working  directly  on  the  raw  text  passed  through  different  layers  of  a  neural  network,  are  able  to  evidence  predictions  about  time  and  space,  in  a  way  which  is  very  close  to  how  humans  do  themselves  when  reading.  In  this  survey  we  will  inventarize  a  number  of  theories,  models  and  techniques  proposed  to  deal  with  time  and  space  in  texts,  with  the  intention  to  lay  the  basis  of  further  research  that  should  focus  on  the  temporal  interpretation  of  the  natural  language.   Classical  NLP  considers  time  from  a  logical  perspective,  rather  distant  from  how  should  it  be  deciphered  in  a  text.  For  
instance,  Fig.  1  displays  the  basic  actions  that  must  be  supported  by  any  formalism  or  model  that  allows  time  processing,  as  objectivised  in  the  general  theory  of  space    and  time  of  J.  Allen   [1]  .

In  order  to  be  processed  the  text  should  be  regarded  as  a  set  of  chronologically  and  logically  connected  events  or  a  set  of  words  that  are  linked  through  relations.  The  relations  can  be  extended  from  words  to  sentences  or  even  paragraphs,  but  also  from  one  event  to  another.  Some  models  describing  this  behaviour  will  be  further  presented.  In  the  following  sections  we  will  revise  TimeML  and  SpatialML,  specification  languages  used  in  natural  language  processing  to  identify  and  mark  events  and  temporal  or  spatial  expressions,  but  also  the  Time  Yards  Model  that  allows  the  segmentation  of  the  text  in  order  to  interpret  each  part  and  correlate  it  with  the  others  through  relations.

  The  temporal  processing  part  is  done  using  the  TimeML  standard,  a  widely  spread  standard  that  has  great  support  and  flexibility  for  processing  temporal  expressions,  events  along  with  their  relations  with  each  other.  This  standard  is  better  than  the  others  because  of  the  large  number  of  libraries  that  offers  support  for  temporal  text  interpretation  in  the  specified  format.  One  of  the  libraries  we  will  use  is  SUTime  which  will  be  presented  further  in  detail.  The  extraction  of  temporal  events  will  help  build  different  types  of  time  tracks  using  the  Time  Yards  Model,  ordering  the  events  and  assigning  them  to  actors.

    TimeML  [2]  is  a  markup  language  for  temporal  information  in  natural  language  texts  and  has  become  an  ISO  standard  [3].    This  language  introduces  temporal  functions  to  express  a  time  interval  more  precisely  ( two  weeks  before,  yesterday)  [4].  The  three  categories  of  time  expressions  that  can  be  expressed  are:  durations  ( two  hours ),  underspecified  temporal  expressions  ( Monday )  and  fully  specified  time  expressions  ( December  2nd  1989 ).  This  standard  has  been  used  widely  in  many  annotated  resources  for  its  flexibility  in  expressing  temporal  events.  It  uses  specific  prepositions  and  connectors  found  in  the  text  as  signals  for  interpretation  ( during,  after,  before ).  It  allows  to  notate  correlations  between  events  and  time  by  using  techniques  such  as  anchoring  one  event  to  another,  ordering  the  events  from  a  temporal  point  of  view.  The  available  corpora  annotated  in  TimeML  standard  are  numerous.  The  corpora  used  to  train  the  prediction  algorithm  in  an  incipient  phase  are  the  TimeBank  1.2  and  the  TimeBank  1.1  corpuses,  as  well  as  the  corpuses  AQUAINT  TimeML,  TempEval  and  TempEval2.  [5][6]   Four  main  XML  data  structures  are  used  in  this  model  to  mark  temporal  elements.    1)  EVENT   Events  describe  actions  that  can  occur  or  have  already  happened.  They  are  usually  associated  to  words  in  a  sentence,  mainly  verbs,  and  have  the  corresponding  tag  <EVENT>.    There  are  several  attributes  that  can  be  specified  for  this  tag,  but  the  most  important  will  be  presented  further.  
 The  Class  attribute  can  have  the  following  values:  Reporting,  Perception,  Aspectual,  I_action,  I_state,  State  and  Occurrence.  These  values  can  offer  important  information  about  the  narration  and  it’s  subjectivity,  helping  us  map  different  plans  of  action  with  each  character  and  see  from  whose  point  of  view  the  story  is  told  and  about  whom  the  action  is  referring  to.  This  kind  of  example  will  be  also  used  to  build  a  prediction  algorithm,  taking  into  account  different  syntax  patterns.     She  <EVENT  eid="e1"  class="REPORTING">claims</EVENT>  that  Elian  is  afraid  to  <EVENT  eid="e2"  class="PERCEPTION">see</EVENT>  his  father.    He  has  <EVENT  eid="e3"  class="OCCURRENCE">made</EVENT>  the  right  <EVENT  eid="e4"  class="OCCURRENCE">decision</EVENT>  coming  up  to  Paris.   His  parents  will  have  to  <EVENT  eid="e5"   class="I_STATE">consider</EVENT>  punishing  him,  otherwise  he  will  be  <EVENT  eid="e39"  class="I_ACTION">resisting</EVENT>  future  disciplination.   The  POS  attribute  can  have  the  following  values:   Verb,  Noun,  Adjective,  Prep  or  Other,  describing  the  main  parts-of-speech  used  in  a  sentence.     The  Tense  attribute  can  have  the  value  of  Past,  Present,  Future,  Infinitive,  Prespart  (ending  in  -ing),  Pastpart  (as  on  verbs  ending  in  -en)  and  None.    The  Aspect  attribute  can  have  the  values  of  Progressive,  Perfective,  Perfective-Progressive  and  None,  referring  to  certain  temporal  properties  of  the  action  (it  is  still  ongoing  or  it  has  already  been  finished).   The  Tense  and  Aspect  attributes  are  very  useful  because  they  offer  information  about  the  duration  or  the  temporal  localization  of  an  event  and  where  it  is  placed  on  a  general  temporal  axis.  Therefore,  we  can  define  temporal  segments  for  each  character  that  has  an  event  linked  to  it  and  can  overlap  events  in  order  to  understand  the  relationships  between  characters.   The  Polarity  attribute  has  a  value  of  zero  or  one  and  it  indicates  the  negative  or  positive  form  of  the  event  (the  verb  from   “ I  haven’t  left  yet”  is  in  its  negative  form,  so  the  polarity  will  be  set  to  zero).   

===PAGE 3===
The  Modality  attribute  is  similar  to  the  Polarity ,  meaning  that  it  has  a  value  of  zero  or  one  and  indicates  the  presence  of  a  modal  verb  (should,  could,  would,  may,  have  to).   The  EVENT  tag  is  therefore  very  important  because  it  can  classify  events  (usually  action  verbs)  through  a  series  of  characteristics  with  very  precise  values  [7].    A.  Events  denoted  by  verbs   All  verbal  predicates  express  an   event  and  can  be  marked  up  as  such,  including  those  which  denote  states.    The  book  [ is ]  on  the  table.  He  [ considers ]  going  to  summer  school.   B.  Events  denoted  by  nouns   Nouns  which  can  express  an  event  have  to  appear  in  at  least  two  of  the  following  contexts:   ●  The  exam  [ lasted  for  a  few  hours ].  ●  The  gallery  [ will  take  place ]  in  Iasi.    C.  Events  denoted  by  adjectives    Although  adjectives  are  usually  used  to  express  features  or  properties,  they  can  also  express  the  nature  of  an  event.   He  has  an  [ unbearable ]  pain  to  his  neck  since  the  accident.  He  had  a  [ furious ]  reaction  when  he  saw  that  his  house  was  robbed.   D.  Events  denoted  by  other  elements   Those  events  who  are  linked  to  other  events  and  are  usually  introduced  by  here  or  there  as  a  predicative  complement  can  be  marked  as  events.    We  are  [ here ]  because  of  his  irrational  actions.   They  are  [ there ]  to  have  a  great  time.    2)  TIMEX3    TIMEX3  is  usually  used  when  we  want  to  annotate  specific  temporal  expressions  (moments  and  periods  of  
time).  The  beginning,  ending,  duration  or  time  of  an  event  is  annotated  with  this  tag.    There  are  three  categories  of  time  that  can  be  interpreted  in  TIMEX3:  fully  specified  interval  or  dates  ( 2nd  February  2019 ),  underspecified  intervals  ( Monday )  or  duration  ( six  hours ).   3)  SIGNAL   Signal  is  usually  used  for  marking  the  temporal  relationship  between  events.  The  words  that  are  annotated  with  <SIGNAL>  are  mainly  words  that  express  a  temporal  order  of  the  events,  such  as:  on,  before,  after  [4].
  
In  TimeML,  Signal  has  been  improved  and  supports  now  the  annotation  of  conditional  temporal  events  ( if )  or  another  type  of  quantifications  ( once,  twice ).   4)  LINK   There  are  several  types  of  links  that  are  supported  in  TimeML:  ALINK,  TLINK,  SLINK.  They  are  precisely  useful  and  innovative  by  allowing  the  annotation  of  events  relationships.  The  link  is  done  through  the  ID  of  events.  [7]  
 
●  ALINK   ALINK  comes  from  Aspectual  Link  and  marks  an  event  that  is  an  aspect  of  another  event  or  it  refers  to  it  ( I  started  reading )  [8].   ●  TLINK    TLINK  comes  from  Temporal  Link  and  marks  a  temporal  relationship  between  events  or  the  positioning  in  time  of  the  events.    Types  of  positioning  can  be:  one  event  before  the  other,  one  event  occurring  in  the  exact  same  time  with  another,  one  event  after  another,  one  event  finished  with  another,  one  event  triggering  the  start  of  another  etc  (see  Fig.  1).    ●  SLINK   SLINK  comes  from  Subordination  Link  and  is  usually  used  whenever  an  event  instance  subordinates  another  event  instance  type   [9] .  ( If  Graham  leaves  today,  he  will  not  hear  Sabine.)

B.   SUT
IME
   SUTime  is  a  library  developed  by  Stanford  University  to  recognize  and  normalize  time  expressions.  It  detects  temporal  expressions  and  computes  a  timespan  between  that  moment  and  the  current  time  reference.  For  example,  the  expression  “next  Wednesday  at  3  p.m.”  will  be  translated  as  “2019-06-25T15:00”  depending  on  the  current  date  and  time.  The  system  has  a  deterministic  rule-based  designed  for  extensibility  and  flexibility.  [11]    In  order  to  identify  the  temporal  expressions  based  on  the  predefined  rules  for  each  language,  SUTime  uses  RegexTokens,  a  framework  that  helps  mapping  patterns  found  in  text  with  semantic  objects.  The  main  types  of  algorithms  used  for  recognising  and  normalising  expressions  are:  1.  text  regex  rules  -  temporal  expressions  are  mapped  with  simple  regex  over  characters;  2.  compositional  rules  -  temporal  expressions  are  mapped  using  regular  expressions  over  chunks  (tokens  and  temporal  objects)  
3.  filtering  rules  -  ambiguous  expressions  are  removed  from  the  candidates  list    The  four  temporal  types  corresponding  also  to  the  TIMEX3  standard:    Time :  Indicates  a  time  point  on  a  particular  timescale.  It  recognizes  both  relative  and  absolute  times.

  The  model  allows  text  segmentation  and  classification  and  uses  the  general  actions  described  in  Table  1  and  associates  them  to  actors.  The  notion  of  time  segments  is  introduced  and  used  here.  A  time  segment  can  be  described  as  a  sequence  of  events  that  can  evolve  linearly  or  not  at  all  and  are  narrated  uninterruptedly  in  a  span  of  text  [13].    There  are  three  ways  to  describe  the  flow  of  time  in  a  text:  ●  Time  passes  only  forward.  This  type  is  used  in  typical  narrations  where  the  action  can  only  happen  in  the  present,  in  the  past,  or  in  the  future,  with  respect  to  the  time  of  narration.  ●  Time  flows  forward  and  backwards,  involving  temporal  switches  between  present  and  past.  This  type  is  used  when  the  narrator  recalls  some  memories  (flashbacks).  ●  Time  can  temporarily  switch  between  present  and  future.  This  is  usually  occurring  when  the  narrator  is  expressing  his  dreams,  hopes,  suppositions  etc.    The  main  purpose  of  this  model  is  to  offer  flexibility  when  it  comes  to  switching  temporal  intervals  from  the  present,  past  or  future.   
 Segmenting  the  text  into  time  segments   can  be  done  using  clues  such  as  main  verbs,  discourse  markers,  indirect  speech  as  indicated  by  quotation  marks.  Segmentation  should  detect  time  segments,  which  are  contiguous  text  spans  made  of  sequences  of  events.  A  time  segment  is  characterised  by  a  stable  set  of  actors  or  just  one  actor,  by  a  spatial  assignment  (if  any)  that  links  the  events  to  a  unique  location  or  a  contiguous  set  of  locations  (like  in  a  journey)  and  whose  timing  evolves  over  one  or  a  contiguous  set  of  time  intervals.     The  other  important  concept  in  the  model  is  the  time  track  which  consists  of  a  time-ordered  sequence  of  time  segments  filtered  only  by  the  involved  actors.  The  intention  of  time  tracks  is  to  describe  “destinies”  of  characters,  their  evolution  in  time  and  space  in  the  story.  When  more  characters  act  at  the  same  time  and  space,  their  time  tracks  intersect.  When  the  same  sequence  of  events  is  narrated  by  more  than  one  narrator,  the  corresponding  time  track  splits  in  parallel  paths.   The  Time  Yard  is  thus  described  as  a  set  of  time  tracks,  each  one  corresponding  to  a  specific  character.  It  can  also  be  visualised  in  diagrams  that  put  in  evidence  the  characters’  interactions  and  relationships.

  The  spatial  processing  part  is  done  using  the  SpatialML  standard,  a  widely  spread  standard  that  offers  support  for  spatial  text  interpretation.  We  will  use  it  because  of  the  high  number  of  libraries  that  use  this  format  to  express  spatial  expressions. This  will  be  very  useful  as  well  when  the  time  tracks  will  be  built  because  each  entity  can  be  located  both  by  time  and  space  in  a  span  of  text,  increasing  the  expressivity. AMED
 
ENTITY
 
RECOGNITION
   Named-entity  recognition  (NER)  (also  known  as  entity  identification,  entity  chunking  and  entity  extraction )  is  a  subtask  of  information  extraction  that  locates  and  classifies  named  entity  mentions  in  texts  into  predefined  categories  such  as  the  person  names,  organizations  and  locations.    Today,  NER  systems  use  linguistic  grammar-based  techniques  as  well  as  statistical  model.  Although  grammar-based  systems   have  better  precision,  they  require  a  large  amount  of  manually  annotated  training  datasets.   Semi  Supervised  approaches  have  been  suggested  to  avoid  part  of  the  annotation  effort.   The  Name  Entity  Recognizer  we  used  is  a  Java  implementation  from  Stanford  University  that  allows  the  annotation  of  persons,  organisations  and  locations.  It  has  been  built  for  feature  extraction  and  has  many  options  for  configuring  them.  It  can  be  used  programmatically  via  an  API  or  a  service.  It  suggests  potential  tags  depending  on  the  selected  configuration  such  as:  location,  organisation,  facility,  date,  money,  people,  percent,  time  [15].     This  framework  will  prove  itself  very  useful  when  extracting  data  from  new  texts.  If  there  aren’t  any  matching  correspondents  in  the  database  for  a  word,  this  framework  will  be  used  to  partially  classify  it  by  knowing  its  type.  Therefore  the  word  can  be  assigned  to  a  class  or  tag  and  the  rest  of  the  attributes  will  be  set  manually.

EMANTIC
 
PROCESSING
 
  
 Semantic  processing  occurs  when  a  word  is  heard  and  is  followed  by  the  attempt  to  encode  it’s  meaning.  The  word  is  usually  placed  in  a  context  mentally  and  allows  people  to  process  deeper  the  message.  Therefore,  semantic  processing  results  in  long  lasting  memories,  compared  to  shallow  processing,  which  only  produces  fragile  and  decayable  memories,  because  it  requires  the  listener  to  think  about  the  meaning  of  a  word.  G
RAMMARS
   A  goal  of  the  research  would  be  to  correlate  syntax  with  semantics  within  dependency  grammar.  This  can  be  done  using  a  tool  named  Treeops  that  converts  an  XML  format  to  another  following  a  set  of  rules  [16].    Previous  works  include  the  UAIC  Ro-Dia  Dependency  Treebank,  which  consists  of  partly  automatically  annotated  and  manually  annotated  sentences,  as  well  as  their  syntactic  and  semantic  correspondence.    The  annotated  data  was  divided  into  14  types  of  circumstantial  modifiers:  concession ,  condition ,  consecutive ,  cumulative ,  causal ,  exception ,  instrumental ,  local ,  modal ,  opposition ,  relative  (or  referential ),  purpose ,  associative  and  temporal .  The  corresponding  semantic  tags  for  these  are  CNCS ,  COND ,  CSQ ,  CUMUL ,  CAUS ,  EXCP ,  INSTR ,  LOC ,  MOD ,  OPPOS ,  REFR ,  PURP ,  ASSOC ,  and  TEMP .   Only  unambiguously  data  can  be  annotated  automatically,  the  rest  being  annotated  manually.  An  example  of  semantic,  syntactic  ambiguity  that  can  be  found  is  I  saw  a  man  on  a  hill  with  a  telescope ,   Look  at  the  dog  with  one  eye .   The  examples  above  are  ambiguous  because  they  are  modifiers  that  specify  purpose,  location  or  temporal  meanings  so  they  were  annotated  as  an   expression.   The  training  data  consists  of  a  collection  of  long  sentences  that  have  the  structure  of  a  tree  where  the  leaves  (annotated  parts  of  the  sentence)  are  linked  between  them  through  operators  (that  resemble  logical  operators,  such  as  conjunction,  implication  etc.).     QuoVadis  is  a  corpus  that  includes  semantically  annotated  relations  from  texts  belonging  to  the  belletrist  genre.  The  annotation  was  realized  in  two  steps:  the  preprocessing  part,  where  parts  of  speech  and  lemmas  were  annotated  automatically  and  the  manual  annotation  part,  

===PAGE 7===
done  by  well-trained  annotators,  in  which  the  entity  and  relation  layers  are  built.    The  entity  layer  focuses  on  the  retrieval  of  information  about  persons,  places,  objects,  ideas  etc.  in  the  text,  while  the  relation  layer  focuses  on  the  relation  between  them  (seen  as  binary  relations).  The  relations  taken  into  account  are  anaphoric,  affectionate,  kinship  and  social.  The  main  purpose  is  to  mark  these  relations  between  individuals  belonging  to  the  following  types:  persons,  gods,  groups  of  people  and  gods  or  part  of  bodies  of  persons  and  gods.  In  order  to  keep  the  subjective  interpretation  to  a  minimum  level  the  texts  are  free  of  unuttered  elements.  The  time  moments  or  their  connection  with  the  entities  are  not  surprised  in  this  corpus  [17].    The  annotating  conventions  will  be  presented  further  in  detail.    1)   Layers  of  annotation    The  first  step  is  to  pre-process  the  text  by  segmenting  it  to  the  sentence  level  (the  boundaries  of  the  sentence  are  marked),  followed  by  tokenization  (words,  numbers,  punctuation,  abbreviations  etc.  are  marked).  The  next  phase  is  to  identify  parts  of  speech  (morpho-syntactic  information  or  POS  categories),  followed  by  lemmatization.  The  last  part  of  the  pre-processing  phase  is  the  noun  phrase  chunking  where  the  previously  extracted  data  receives  additional  information  about  noun  boundaries  or  head  words  [18].    2)    Entity  annotation    The  next  step  is  to  annotate  entities  with  the  XML  tag  ENTITY .  An  entity  is  usually  associated  with  a  noun  phrase  at  the  text  level  and  could  be  represented  on  a  semantic  level  as  a  container  (occasionally,  even  empty),  where  identification  details  contributed  by  the  text,  such  as:  name,  sex,  religion,  beliefs  etc.,  could  be  filled  in.  The  purpose  of  this  representation  is  to  help  the  process  of  anaphora  resolution.  The  attention  is  focused  on  annotation  persons  or  gods,  but  also  person-groups,  person-class  (representing  occupations  or  topologies),  person-names  (representing  the  person’s  name)  and  person-part  (identifying  human  anatomical  parts).   The  realisation  of  entities  on  syntactic  level  is  done  through  nominal  phrases  (NP).  They  are  called  referential  expressions  (RE)  when  are  used  to  recall  from  memory  certain  containers  and  help  gathering  additional  information.   
 Nominal  phrases  are  used  when  interpreting  syntactic  properties  and  referential  expressions  are  used  when   interpreting  semantic  properties  and  text  coverage.  Nominal  phrases  can  include  other  nominal  phrases,  but  they  usually  have  different  heads  and  thus  represent  different  entities.  In  order  to  be  useful  for  training  NP-chunkers  the  referential  expressions  annotate  the  whole  NP-construction  and  not  only  their  head.    When  several  imbricated  nominal  expressions  have  the  same  head  only  the  longest  will  be  annotated.  For  example,  in  the  construction  “[other  women  of  [the  high  society]]”  is  annotated  altogether  and  not  separately  as  “[other  [women]  of  [the  high  society]]”  or  “[other  [women  of  [the  high  society]]]”  because  all  three  choices  have  the  same  head  (“women”)  and  only  the  longest  is  annotated.   3)    Relation  annotation     The  main  purpose  of  these  types  of  annotation  is  to  put  in   evidence  the  minimal  span  of  text  from  which  any  user  can  decipher  a  relation.  Relations  are  considered  to  have  two  poles  (“from”  and  “to”)  that  can  intersect  and  are  triggered  by  a  word  or  an  expression.  If  the  poles  intersect,  the  larger  entity  is  considered  “from”  and  the  smaller  one  is  considered  “to”.  For  example  in  the  construction  “1:[to  the  second  husband  2:[of  Popeea]”  ([1]  spouse-of  [2])  the  “from”  pole  is  the  first  expression  and  the  “to”  pole  is  the  second  [19].   4)    Referential  relations    This  type  of  annotation  is  used  to  mark  a  relation  between  a  referential  expression  and  an  entity  (the  container  and  it’s  gathered  information).  The  main  types  of  referential  relations  are:  member-of  (a  person  or  a  god  is  a  member  of  person-group  or  god-group),  has-a-member  (the  opposite  of  member-of ),  isa  (from  a  person  or  god  to  a  person-class  or  a  god-class),  class-of  (the  opposite  of  isa ),  part-of  (person-part  or  god-part  is  linked  to  a  body  part  of  a  person  or  a  god),  has-a-part  (the  opposite  of  part-of ),  subgroup-of  (from  a  person-group  or  god-group  to  a  larger  person-group  or  god-group)  and  name-of  (representing  the  name  of  a  person  or  a  god).   5)    Kinship  relations    This  type  of  annotation  is  used  to  evidentiate  the  link  between  two  referential  expressions  represented  by  a  person,  a  god,  a  person-group  or  a  god-group.  The  main  types  of

The  main  types  of  affective  relations  are:  friend-of  (symmetric),  fear-of ,  fear-to ,  love,  loved-by ,  rec-love  (mutual  love  relation),  hate ,  hated-by ,  upset-on ,  worship  and  worshiped-by.   7)     Social  relations      This  type  of  annotations  is  used  to  represent  non-anaphoric  relations  between  persons  or  person-groups. LEARNING
    The  main  purpose  of  the  research  is  to  be  able  to  perform  semi-supervised  annotations.  For  several  decades,  statisticians  have  tried  to  combine  labeled  and  unlabeled  data  in  order  to  train  classifiers  and  to  develop  a  generative  model  for  semi-supervised  learning.  Developing  semi-supervised  learning  algorithms  is  necessary  because  the  manually  annotation  process  is  very  time  consuming  and  because  there  already  exists  a  large  amount  of  labeled  data  that  can  be  used,  but  also  because  of  the  increasingly  unlabeled  data  [24].     The  classification  methods  used  in  supervised  learning  include  the  generative  and  discriminative  models.   The  generative  models  computes  the  joint  probability  distribution.  Given  a  variable  X  and  its  target  variable  Y  the  generative  models  will  compute  P(X,Y)  [25].    The  discriminative  model  differs  from  the  previous  because  the  joint  probability  distribution  does  not  have  to  be  computed.  Given  a  variable  X  and  a  target  Y  the  discriminative  model  will  compute  the  conditional   probability  P(X|Y)  [26].   
 One  of  the  approaches  used  in  semi-supervised  learning  is  to  use  expectation-maximization  algorithm  (EM)  applied  on  generative  models  such  as  Naive  Bayes,  considering  unlabeled  data  as  data  that  has  missing  information  [27].  The  EM  algorithm  is  an  iterative  method  (mathematical  procedure  that  generates  a  solution  to  a  problem  where  each  term  is  obtained  from  the  previous  one)  that  estimated  parameters  in  statistical  models  by  finding  the  maximum  likelihood.  The  EM  algorithm  alternated  between  the  expectation  and  maximization  step  on  each  iteration.  The  expectation  step  consists  in  creating  a  function  to  compute  the  log-likelihood  using  the  current  iteration’s  parameter  values.  The  maximization  step  consists  in  maximizing  the  function  from  the  previous  step  and  find  the  function  parameters  [28].     Discriminative  models  are  used  lately  in  many  text  classification  tasks  because  they  don’t  need  to  compune  the  joint  probability  distributions  [29].     Another  approach  is  to  use  a  discriminative  classifier  such  as  the  maximum  entropy  algorithm  (MaxEnt)  because  of  its  tractability  and  easy  optimisation,  computing  the  conditional  probability  of  a  label  given  a  set  of  features  [30].     This  model  is  a  starting  point  for  different  semi-supervised  learning  techniques,  such  as  the  one  proposed  by  Grandvalet  et  al.  which  performs  an  entropy  regularization  by  adding  another  term  to  the  MaxEnt  training  objective,  encouraging  a  lower  entropy  for  the  predicted  labels   [31].    Another  model  exploring  the  MaxEnt  algorithm  is  called  expectation-regularization  (XR)  that  also  adds  a  regularization  term  to  the  training  objective.  It  has  been  proven  that  XR  is  better  at  some  classification  tasks  than  the  entropy  minimization  [32].     A.  Semi-Supervised  PLSA    Another  model  that  is  used  to  capture  polysemy  and  synonymy  in  texts  is  the  Probabilistic  Latent  Semantic  Analysis  (PLSA)  model.  This  model  extends  the  EM  model  and  uncovers  the  relation  between  labels  and  features.  Fake  labels  are  added  to  the  unlabeled  data  to  avoid  assigning  arbitrary  classes  and  make  arbitrary  decisions  during  the  classification  [33].    


===PAGE 10===
 A  latent  variable  is  associated  with  each  occurrence  of  a  word  aiming  to  account  for  polysemy  and  discover  the  topic  of  the  text.  All  labeled  data  will  keep  their  label  and  the  unlabeled  will  receive  a  label  equal  to  zero.    By  splitting  the  last  equation  in  two  terms,  one  for  the  labeled  data  and  one  for  the  unlabeled  data,  we  obtain  an  equation  where  the  first  part  of  the  equation  can  be  maximized  analytically,  but  the  second  part  requires  applying  the  EM  algorithm  due  to  the  presence  of  the  sum  under  the  log.   Once  the  parameters  are  set  via  a  fixed  point  of  EM,  each  variable  x  we  can  obtain  the  probability  of  being  fake  or  not.   Therefore  we  can  classify  unlabeled  data  using  PLSA  and  EM. B.  Active  learning     Active  learning  (or  query  learning)  is  a  special  type  of  machine  learning  where  the  unlabeled  data  is  easily  classified  using  queries,  allowing  the  training  set  to  be  selected  dynamically  when  needed  in  order  to  reduce  the  annotation  burden  and  time-consuming  process   [35].    This  type  of  learning  has  great  results  even  with  fewer  labeled  data  if  the  training  set  can  be  specified  via  an  oracle  (ex.  a  human  annotator).  It  is  usually  used  when  unlabeled  data  is  available  in  large  quantities  but  the  labeled  data  is  limited  and  expensive  to  obtain.  There  are  many  types  of   active  learning  scenarios,  but  in  this  article  we  will  consider  the  pool-based  sampling  because  it  best  fits  the  description:  small  sets  of  labeled  data  are  available  and  large  sets  of  unlabeled  data  are  available  and  can  be  gathered  at  once.   Usually  the  pool  is  considered  to  be  closed,  but  not  necessarily.  Queries  are  chosen  from  the  pool  in  a  greedy  way  based  on  the  level  of  information  they  provide.  The  pool-based  approach  has  been  used  successfully  in  text  classification  [36],  speech  recognition  [37],  image  and  video  classification  and  retrieval  [38][39]  and  many  other  domains  that  involve  large  datasets  of  unlabeled  data.    This  learning  cycle  is  described  in  Fig.  5.  The  oracle  in  this  case  will  be  a  trained  supervisor  (ex.  a  trained  user  of  the  platform).  The  oracle  will  interfere  in  the  classification  process  only  for  those  instances  who  are  most  uncertain  to  classify  by  the  model.  The  cycle  starts  from  a  labeled  training  set  on  which  a  machine  learning  model  is  applied  in  order  to  extract  the  necessary  information.  The  next  step  is  to  go  through  the  unlabeled  set  and  choose  those  queries  that  can  not  be  classified  with  good  accuracy  in  order  to  be  manually  classified  by  the  oracle.

Uncertainty  Sampling    Uncertainty  Sampling  is  probably  one  of  the  most  common  and  easy  methods  used  in  pool-based  approach.  The  strategy  behind  supposes  that  the  active  learner  is  querying  only  those  instances  of  which  he  is  the  most  uncertain  of. Each  member  of  the  committee  has  a  vote  regarding  the  label  of  the  current  query.  The  query  on  which  the  most  disagree  is  the  most  informative  one  for  the  active  learner. This  will  result  in  better  precision  even  with  less  training  instances  [41].

   This  paper  proposes  a  model  of  semi-supervised  annotation  using  the  Ro  Time-Bank  corpus  and  manual  annotations. The  goal  of  this  paper  is  to  create  a  visual  representation  of  all  characters  from  a  temporal  and  spatial  point  of  view.  In  order  to  achieve  that,  each  person  will  be  mapped  to  a  pair  of  coordinates:  x  -  representing  time  and  y  -  representing  space.     The  4  types  of  text  segmentation  are:   A.  Spatial     A  spatial  axis  will  align  all  characters  in  different  spatial  plans,  allowing  us  to  see  which  ones  can  interact  or  have  interacted  at  some  point  in  the  past  based  on  their  location.   B.  Temporal     A  temporal  axis  will  align  the  characters,  more  precisely  their  actions,  offering  a  perspective  about  their  evolution  over  time  as  well  as  their  interactions  or  other  relations  between  them  (such  as  age  difference  etc.).   C.  Character     The  same  way  we  aligned  all  characters  on  a  general  spatial  and  temporal  axis,  we  can  keep  track  of  the  evolution  of  a  character,  when  building  its  own  space  and  time  axis.  If  each  character  has  its  own  coordinates  on  the  axis,  their  representations  can  be  joined  in  a  unified  one  to  obtain  a  general  view  of  their  interactions.  
D.  Perspective    Often,  the  same  development  of  events  is  narrated  from  different  perspectives,  for  instance,  the  author  and  one  or  more  characters.  The  perspective  is  thus  very  important  in  understanding  the  relations  between  characters  and  in  building  the  space-time  axis.  The  narrator  can  have  a  different  understanding  of  time  than  the  characters  he  is  talking  about,  so  reconstructing  these  different  representations  gives  another  dimension  of  the  novel’s  structure.    In  order  to  do  this,  the  machinery  to  interpret  and  decipher  the  text  should  also  be  able  to  add  a  perspective  label  for  each  text  segment.  As  such,  the  text  will  be  analyzed  not  only  with  respect  to  time,  space  and  involved  characters,  but  also  from  the  narrators’  perspectives,  thus  generating  different  narrative  plans.     In  this  report,  we  have  presented  a  few  models  that  we  intend  to  use  in  our  research,  aiming  at  describing  in  computational  terms  the  temporal,  semantic  and  syntactic  interpretation  of  a  text.  In  particular,  we  have  described  two  formalisms  that  specify  very  precisely  the  relation  between  events  through  a  series  of  temporal  positioning  actions  ( X  before  Y,  X  simultaneously  with  Y  etc.).  Combining  this  level  of  time  annotation  with  time  segments,  we  arrive  at  time  tracks,  which  contain  action  developments  of  characters  aligned  within  a  discourse  time  axis  that  combines  the  past,  present  and  future,  as  figured  out  in  the  text.  Future  work  will  examine  the  use  of  the  proposed  models  in  order  to  temporarily  anchor  and  order  events  in  news  articles  and  fiction  texts  that  narrate  temporal  evolutions.   English  is  known  to  be  extremely  rich  in  free  annotated  texts,  while  similar  resources  for  the  Romanian  language  are  hard  to  find.  As  a  result,  we  intend  to  dedicate  a  part  of  our  work  towards  the  creation  of  annotated  Romanian  texts,  in  the  annotation  languages  that  we  revised  in  this  report,  this  way  bringing  a  minor  contribution  to  the  scientific  evolution  of  our  language.  However,  the  formalisms  that  we  intend  to  develop  should  be  general  enough  to  be  applicable  to  different  languages.  Because  of  the  lack  of  platforms  that  allow  users  to  easily  annotate  texts  (manually  or  semi-supervised),  this  research  has  considered  developing  one  that  can  apply  the  mentioned  algorithms  to  identify  temporal  and  spatial  expressions,  to  automatically  annotate  data  that  match  the
